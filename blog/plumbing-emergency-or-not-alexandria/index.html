What Counts as a Plumbing Emergency in Alexandria (And What Can Wait)?

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

One of the most common hesitations homeowners have at 11pm with a plumbing issue is not knowing whether it is worth calling right then or whether it can wait until a normal business hour. Getting this wrong in either direction has real costs: calling unnecessarily for something that could wait means paying more than you needed to, while waiting on something that genuinely needed immediate attention can mean significantly worse damage by morning.

Clear Emergencies: Call Immediately

What to do if you have one of these right now

  • Active water spraying, pooling, or flowing from a pipe. Shut off the main water valve if you can safely reach it, then call. Every minute this continues adds to the eventual damage.
  • Sewage backing up into the home. This is both a property damage issue and a genuine health consideration, and it should never wait until morning.
  • No water at all in the entire house. This can indicate a main line failure or a well pump issue that needs prompt attention, particularly if it happened suddenly.
  • A gas smell near any plumbing or water heater. Leave the home and call your gas utility's emergency line first, then a plumber once the immediate gas safety concern is addressed.
  • A frozen pipe that has just cracked and is now leaking. Once ice has already caused a crack, this becomes an active leak situation, not just a freeze to monitor.

Genuine Gray Areas

Some situations are less clear-cut and depend on specifics. A water heater with no hot water is inconvenient but rarely an emergency unless it is actively leaking, in which case the leak, not the lack of hot water, is what makes it urgent. A single slow drain is almost never an emergency, even if it is annoying, unless it is backing up and threatening to overflow. A running toilet wastes water and should be addressed reasonably soon, but it is not something that needs a 2am call unless the tank is actively overflowing onto the floor.

The general test worth applying: is water actively escaping somewhere it should not be right now, or is this a problem that exists but is not actively getting worse by the minute? The former is an emergency. The latter can typically wait for standard business hours, when non-emergency rates and scheduling apply.

What Can Almost Always Wait

A dripping faucet, a slow drain that still drains, a toilet that runs occasionally but not constantly, low water pressure that has been gradually declining rather than suddenly dropping, and cosmetic issues like a loose fixture handle are all things that can reasonably wait for a scheduled, non-emergency appointment. None of these are actively causing additional damage by waiting a day or two, and scheduling them during normal hours is typically less expensive than an after-hours emergency call.

Why This Distinction Actually Matters Financially

Emergency and after-hours dispatch, by its nature, costs more than a scheduled daytime appointment, since it involves a technician responding outside normal working hours. Homeowners who understand this distinction save money by correctly identifying which of their situations are genuine emergencies versus which are inconvenient but stable problems that can be scheduled normally. At the same time, hesitating on a genuine emergency to avoid an after-hours rate is usually the more expensive mistake, since the resulting water damage often costs far more than the difference between emergency and standard pricing would have.

When in Doubt, Call and Ask

If you are genuinely unsure which category your situation falls into, calling and describing what you are seeing is a reasonable step even outside business hours. A dispatcher or technician can help you assess whether the specific situation warrants immediate response or can reasonably be scheduled for the next available appointment, without committing you to emergency pricing before that assessment happens.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does calling after hours always mean emergency pricing? Typically yes, if a technician is dispatched outside normal hours. However, describing your situation first can help determine whether immediate dispatch is genuinely necessary or whether the issue can reasonably wait for standard scheduling.

What if I'm not sure and it turns out not to be an emergency? This is a reasonable situation to be in, and being cautious about active water damage is generally the right instinct. A brief phone assessment can often clarify this before a technician is actually dispatched.

Is a burst pipe always worse at night than during the day? The urgency is the same regardless of time, since water damage accumulates at the same rate whether it is 2am or 2pm. The difference is simply whether standard or after-hours dispatch and pricing applies.

A Practical Way to Prepare Before You Ever Need This Framework

The best time to think through this decision framework is not in the middle of an actual plumbing problem, when stress and uncertainty make clear thinking harder. Taking a few minutes now, while nothing is wrong, to locate your home's main water shutoff valve and save a plumber's number somewhere easy to find means that if an actual emergency does happen, you are not searching for basic information while water is actively spreading. This kind of preparation costs nothing and pays off exactly once, at precisely the moment it matters most.

How Alexandria's Housing Stock Affects This Calculation

The same emergency-versus-wait framework applies regardless of neighborhood, but the underlying likelihood of certain problems does vary across Alexandria. A homeowner in Old Town or Rosemont with older galvanized plumbing has a somewhat higher baseline chance of encountering a sudden pipe failure than a homeowner in newer construction like Cameron Station or Potomac Yard, simply due to pipe age and material. This does not change what counts as an emergency, active water is always urgent regardless of the home's age, but it is a reasonable factor in deciding how seriously to take smaller warning signs, like a slow leak or unusual pipe noise, before they become an active emergency.

Trusting Your Own Judgment

Homeowners sometimes worry about being wrong in either direction, calling for something that turns out to be minor, or waiting on something that turns out to have been serious. A reasonable approach is to weight your decision toward caution when water is actively visible and moving, and toward patience when a problem is simply present but stable. This framework will not perfectly predict every situation, but it gives you a genuine starting point better than guessing from scratch each time something comes up, and a dispatcher on the phone can always help refine that judgment for your specific situation and what you are actually observing at home.
