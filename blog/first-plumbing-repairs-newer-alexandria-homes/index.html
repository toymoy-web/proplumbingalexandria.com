Cameron Station and Potomac Yard: What to Expect From Your Home's First Major Plumbing Repairs

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

Owning a newer home in Cameron Station or Potomac Yard comes with a reasonable assumption: new construction means fewer plumbing problems, at least for a while. That assumption is generally correct, but "a while" has a real end date, and homeowners in the earliest phases of both communities are starting to reach it.

Why New Doesn't Mean Forever

Cameron Station's first homes were built in 1999 on the site of a former U.S. Army quartermaster depot, with four more construction phases following through the early 2000s. Potomac Yard is newer still, with development running from the 2010s onward and its own Metro station opening in 2023. Neither community has anything close to the galvanized pipe issues that define Old Town or Rosemont, but "modern plumbing" does not mean "plumbing that never needs attention." Water heaters, garbage disposals, and fixture seals all have finite service lives regardless of how new the surrounding construction is.

What's Actually Reaching End of Life First

What to check as your Cameron Station or Potomac Yard home ages

  • Original water heaters. A tank water heater installed during initial construction is typically rated for 8 to 12 years. Cameron Station's earliest phases are now well past that window.
  • Garbage disposals. These typically last 8 to 12 years under normal use, and original units from Cameron Station's early 2000s phases are reaching or past that point.
  • Fixture seals and cartridges. Faucets and shower valves wear out on a similar timeline regardless of home age, and original fixtures now 20-plus years old in Cameron Station are common candidates.
  • Supply line connections at appliances. Washing machine and dishwasher supply lines have a finite lifespan and are worth a visual check as they age, even in a well-maintained home.

Cameron Station's Five Phases Age Differently

Because Cameron Station was built across five distinct phases from 1999 into the early 2000s, not every home in the community is reaching this point at the same time. A homeowner in one of the earliest phases is dealing with genuinely older original equipment than someone in a later phase, even though both are part of the same overall community and were built using similar standards. Knowing which phase your home belongs to gives a more accurate read on what to expect than assuming the whole community ages uniformly.

Potomac Yard Is Still Mostly Ahead of This Curve

Given its more recent construction timeline, most of Potomac Yard has not yet reached the point where original equipment failure is common. The more relevant plumbing consideration here is still new-construction installation as development continues, rather than aging-equipment replacement. That said, the earliest completed sections of Potomac Yard are beginning to approach the age where water heater and fixture attention becomes a reasonable planning consideration, even if it is not yet the dominant call type in the area.

A Proactive Approach Beats a Reactive One

Homeowners who wait for a failure, a water heater that stops producing hot water, a disposal that seizes up entirely, generally end up dealing with the least convenient version of that failure, often discovered at an inconvenient time. A proactive check as original equipment approaches typical end-of-life windows, particularly for water heaters given how disruptive a sudden failure is, tends to be the more manageable path. This does not mean replacing everything preemptively regardless of condition, but it does mean treating the 8 to 12 year mark as a reasonable point to have equipment assessed rather than waiting for an obvious failure.

What This Typically Costs

A proactive water heater assessment costs little relative to the eventual replacement, typically just a standard service call. If replacement is warranted, a tank water heater replacement runs $1,400 to $3,800 depending on unit type. Garbage disposal replacement typically runs $150 to $500. These costs are consistent with what any Alexandria homeowner would pay, regardless of neighborhood, since the equipment itself and its typical service life do not vary by location.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know which construction phase my Cameron Station home is in? This is typically documented in your home's original purchase or HOA records, and a technician familiar with the community can often also give a reasonable estimate based on specific construction details.

Is it normal for a 20-year-old water heater to still be working fine? It happens, but it is operating well past its typical rated lifespan at that point, and the risk of a sudden failure increases correspondingly the longer it continues running.

Should I replace everything at once as my home reaches this age? Not necessarily. A proactive assessment can identify what is genuinely approaching failure versus what still has reasonable remaining service life, which avoids unnecessary replacement of equipment that is still functioning well.

What a Proactive Assessment Actually Looks Like

A proactive plumbing assessment for a home in this age range typically involves checking the water heater's manufacture date and current condition, testing water pressure throughout the house to catch any early signs of a developing issue, and visually inspecting supply line connections at major appliances for wear. This is a modest time investment compared to the disruption of an unexpected failure, and it gives a homeowner a genuinely informed basis for deciding what to address now versus what can reasonably wait another year or two.

For Cameron Station specifically, a technician who has serviced multiple homes across the community's five phases often has a useful reference point for what typical equipment condition looks like at each phase's current age, which adds real value beyond what a generic inspection checklist would provide.

Building a Realistic Maintenance Timeline

Rather than waiting for the first failure to prompt a maintenance mindset, homeowners in both communities benefit from establishing a rough timeline based on their home's actual age. A water heater approaching the 8 to 10 year mark is worth a check-in, even if it seems to be running fine. A garbage disposal in the same age range is worth testing under normal use to confirm it is still operating at full capacity rather than struggling. This kind of proactive timeline, tied to actual equipment age rather than waiting for a symptom, is the difference between planning a replacement on your own schedule and being forced into an emergency decision when something fails without warning, often at the least convenient possible moment.
