Why Old Town Alexandria Basements Flood (And What to Do About It)

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

If you own a home in Old Town Alexandria, you have probably noticed something your friends in newer parts of the region do not deal with: a basement or crawlspace that gets damp, or worse, floods, even when the rain does not seem unusually heavy. This is not a fluke, and it is not a sign your house was built poorly. It is a direct result of where Old Town sits on the map.

The Water Table Is the Real Culprit

Old Town's oldest blocks were laid out in 1749, right along the Potomac River waterfront. That location made it a natural seaport, and it is exactly why so many homes here sit on a naturally high water table. Groundwater is close to the surface across much of the neighborhood, and it pushes against foundation walls and basement floors constantly, not just during storms.

A house on higher, drier ground somewhere else in Northern Virginia might only deal with this kind of pressure once or twice a year. In Old Town, especially on the blocks closest to the river, it is closer to a constant condition. A healthy sump pump usually keeps up with it. A pump that is undersized, aging, or has failed does not, and that is when a homeowner discovers the problem the hard way.

Storm Surge and Tidal Flooding Add a Second Layer

Beyond the everyday groundwater pressure, the lowest blocks near the waterfront carry a real, periodic risk of tidal and storm-surge flooding from the Potomac itself. This is different from a typical inland basement leak. It means water can rise from outside the foundation during a significant storm event, not just seep in from below.

This combination, constant groundwater pressure plus occasional storm-surge risk, is why waterfront blocks in Old Town generate more water damage restoration and sump pump calls than almost any other part of Alexandria.

What Actually Helps

A realistic prevention checklist for Old Town homeowners

  • Know your sump pump's age. Most residential sump pumps last 7 to 10 years under normal duty. A pump running constantly in a high-water-table home wears out faster than that.
  • Consider a battery backup. Power outages often accompany the same storms that cause flooding, which is the worst possible time for a sump pump to lose power.
  • Check the discharge line every season. A blocked or frozen discharge line can make a perfectly good pump useless.
  • Watch for recurring dampness, not just standing water. A musty smell or slight dampness after rain is often the first sign, well before an actual flood.

When to Call a Plumber Versus a Contractor

Not every basement moisture issue needs a plumber. If the problem is water seeping in through a foundation crack with no active pump involved, that can be a waterproofing contractor's job. But if the issue involves a sump pump that is not keeping up, a pit that needs to be resized, or actual standing water that needs to be extracted and dried, that is squarely plumbing and restoration territory.

A quick way to tell the difference: if there is a pump involved, or if water has already accumulated, call a plumber first. They can assess whether the pump itself is the problem or whether the water volume has simply outpaced what any residential pump could reasonably handle.

Real Cost Expectations

Homeowners dealing with recurring flooding often want to know what they are actually facing financially before calling anyone. A straightforward sump pump replacement, sized correctly for a waterfront property, typically runs $450 to $1,800 depending on whether a battery backup is included and whether the existing pit needs any work. If water has already made it into the basement, extraction and drying through a full water damage restoration typically runs $1,300 to $5,500, following the IICRC S500 water damage restoration standard, with the range driven mostly by how much flooring, drywall, and insulation absorbed water before the source was addressed.

The practical takeaway is that the cost of prevention is consistently lower than the cost of cleanup. A homeowner who replaces an aging pump proactively for a few hundred dollars is almost always better off than one who waits for a failure and ends up needing both a new pump and a full restoration after the fact.

A Few Questions Old Town Homeowners Ask Often

Does homeowner's insurance cover this kind of flooding? It depends on the cause. Sudden damage from a failed pump or a burst pipe is often covered. Gradual seepage or rising groundwater without a separate flood policy typically is not. This distinction genuinely matters when filing a claim, so it is worth confirming your specific policy language rather than assuming either way.

Is this really worse in Old Town than the rest of Alexandria? Yes, specifically for the blocks closest to the waterfront. Homes further from the river, even within Old Town, see meaningfully less of this pressure than the streets directly along King Street's eastern end toward the Potomac.

Can anything permanently fix a high water table? Not entirely, since the water table itself is a geographic fact of the location. What can be addressed is how well a home's sump system and, where appropriate, interior drainage handle that reality, which is a very different question from eliminating the water table altogether.

What to Do If You Are Not Sure Where You Stand

If you have never had your sump system assessed and you own a home anywhere near Old Town's waterfront blocks, a straightforward inspection can tell you whether your current setup is adequately sized for the property's real groundwater exposure, whether a battery backup makes sense given your specific flood history, and roughly how much service life the existing pump has left before it becomes the next emergency call. That is a meaningfully better position to be in than discovering the answer during an active storm, when the same information costs far more to learn.

Old Town's combination of historic construction and waterfront geography is not going to change, but how well prepared a specific home is for that reality varies enormously from one address to the next, often based on nothing more than whether the current sump system was ever properly sized in the first place. A pump installed decades ago for a smaller pit, or one that has simply never been evaluated against the property's actual water volume, is a common and entirely fixable gap once someone actually looks at it.
