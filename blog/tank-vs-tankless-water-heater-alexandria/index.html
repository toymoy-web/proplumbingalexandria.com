Tank vs. Tankless Water Heaters for Alexandria's Older Homes

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

Choosing between a traditional tank and a tankless water heater is a decision most homeowners only think through once every decade or so, which makes it easy to rely on generic advice that does not account for what actually matters in an older Alexandria home specifically.

The Basic Tradeoffs

What each option actually offers

  • Tank water heaters cost less upfront, install more simply, and are the familiar, proven option. Their downside is a finite hot water supply and a shorter typical lifespan, generally 10 to 15 years.
  • Tankless water heaters provide effectively endless hot water on demand and typically last 20-plus years, but cost more upfront and require more specific installation conditions, including venting.

Why Venting Is the Real Deciding Factor in Old Town or Rosemont

Generic comparisons often treat venting as a minor installation detail, but in Alexandria's pre-1960s rowhouses, it is frequently the single factor that determines whether a tankless conversion is straightforward or genuinely complicated. Tankless units require specific venting that many older homes were never built to accommodate, since traditional tank water heaters have different, often simpler venting requirements. A conversion from tank to tankless in one of these older homes sometimes requires adding new venting entirely, which adds real cost and complexity beyond just swapping the unit itself.

This does not mean tankless is off the table for an older Alexandria home, but it does mean the venting assessment needs to happen before, not after, deciding to convert, since it directly affects both feasibility and total project cost.

Household Size and Usage Patterns Matter More Than Marketing Suggests

Tankless water heaters are often marketed around the idea of never running out of hot water, which is genuinely true, but the practical benefit depends heavily on household size and usage patterns. A single person or couple who rarely runs multiple hot water fixtures simultaneously may not notice much practical difference from a well-sized tank system. A larger household that regularly runs a shower, a dishwasher, and a washing machine at overlapping times is the scenario where tankless's on-demand capacity provides the most noticeable, real-world benefit.

The Cost Comparison Over Time, Not Just Upfront

A tank water heater installation typically runs $1,400 to $2,600, while a tankless conversion, including any necessary venting work, typically runs $2,800 to $5,500 depending on the home's existing setup. The tankless option costs more upfront, but its lower monthly energy usage and roughly double the service life mean the total cost over a comparable time period is often closer between the two options than the initial price difference suggests. This calculation depends heavily on your specific home's venting situation and household usage, which is exactly why a generic online cost comparison does not substitute for an actual assessment.

What This Looks Like for a Typical Old Town Rowhouse

A typical pre-1960s Old Town or Rosemont rowhouse replacing an aging tank water heater has three realistic paths: replace with another tank, which is simplest and cheapest but keeps the household on a limited hot water supply; convert to tankless with straightforward venting, if the home's layout allows it without major modification; or convert to tankless with new venting required, which is the more expensive path but still deliverable in most cases with the right planning. A technician assessing your specific home can tell you which of these three scenarios actually applies before you commit to a decision.

Making the Decision

For a household that regularly runs out of hot water with their current tank system, or that is already planning a broader renovation where venting changes are more feasible to incorporate, tankless is often worth the additional upfront cost. For a household satisfied with their current tank system's capacity, or facing a venting situation that would make conversion significantly more expensive, a straightforward tank replacement remains a perfectly reasonable choice, not a compromise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can any Old Town home be converted to tankless? Most can, but the venting requirements vary enough by specific home that an assessment is necessary before committing to the conversion, rather than assuming it will be straightforward.

Is tankless worth it for a smaller household? It depends more on whether the household currently runs out of hot water than on size alone. A smaller household with no current hot water complaints may see less practical benefit relative to the added cost.

How long does a tank-to-tankless conversion take? A straightforward conversion with adequate existing venting can often be completed in a single day. One requiring new venting work typically takes longer and is quoted based on the specific scope once assessed.

Energy Costs: A Real But Modest Factor

Tankless units are genuinely more energy-efficient than tank systems, since they only heat water as it is actually used rather than maintaining a full tank of hot water continuously. For a typical household, this translates to real but modest monthly energy savings, not a dramatic difference that alone justifies the higher upfront cost. Homeowners motivated primarily by cost savings should view the energy efficiency as a meaningful but secondary factor, with the more decisive considerations usually being household hot water demand and the home's specific venting situation.

Maintenance Expectations Differ Between the Two Options

A tank water heater requires relatively little routine maintenance beyond periodic sediment flushing, and its failure mode is usually gradual, giving some warning through reduced performance before a full failure. A tankless system requires more deliberate annual maintenance, specifically descaling to manage the mineral buildup that Alexandria's water supply makes a genuine consideration, but rewards that maintenance with a considerably longer service life. Homeowners who prefer a lower-maintenance appliance may lean toward tank systems for that reason alone, independent of the cost and capacity considerations discussed above.

What to Ask Before Committing Either Way

Before finalizing a decision, it is worth asking a technician a few specific questions: what does your home's existing venting actually support without modification, what would a full tankless conversion cost including any necessary venting changes, and given your household's actual hot water usage pattern, would the practical difference between tank and tankless be significant or minor in daily life. These three answers, specific to your home and household rather than generic, are what should ultimately drive the decision, more than any general comparison an article like this one can provide on its own without seeing your actual house. Either option, once installed, is completed to the Virginia Uniform Statewide Building Code's plumbing standards (IPC).
