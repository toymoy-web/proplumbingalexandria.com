Signs Your Rosemont or Beverley Hills Home Has a Root-Damaged Sewer Line

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

If you live in Rosemont or Beverley Hills and your drains have needed cabling more than once this year, there is a good chance the problem is not the drain itself. It is roots, and it is one of the most predictable plumbing issues in these two neighborhoods specifically.

This is not a rare or unusual problem confined to a handful of unlucky addresses. It is a structural reality of these two neighborhoods' shared combination of construction era and deliberate, tree-heavy landscaping, which means a large share of homes in both areas will eventually deal with it in some form.

Why These Two Neighborhoods in Particular

Rosemont was developed as a streetcar suburb between 1908 and 1930, and its more than 450 homes were built along a layout that reflected the era's City Beautiful planning ideals, with generous, tree-lined streets as part of the design. Beverley Hills followed a similar approach in the 1930s, with roughly 600 homes built around deliberately preserved mature oaks and dogwoods. Both neighborhoods still have that original tree canopy today, which is exactly the problem.

Sewer laterals installed during these construction windows were almost always clay or cast iron, materials that were standard at the time but that develop small cracks and joint separations after 90-plus years underground. Tree roots do not create these openings. They simply find them, since roots grow toward any source of moisture, and a leaking sewer joint is exactly that.

The Signs That Point to Roots, Not a Simple Clog

How to tell the difference

  • The same drain clogs again within weeks of being cleared. A standard clog, once cabled, usually stays clear for months or longer. Root intrusion regrows and clogs again on a predictable, shorter cycle.
  • More than one fixture backs up at the same time. If your kitchen sink, tub, and toilet all act up together, the problem is in the main line, not an individual fixture.
  • Gurgling sounds when another fixture runs. Air bubbling back through a drain while a different fixture is in use usually means a partial blockage further down the line.
  • A sewage smell in the yard, especially near where the line runs. This can indicate a joint has separated enough to let odor, and eventually water, escape underground.

Why a Camera Inspection Matters Here Specifically

Because Rosemont and Beverley Hills homes share this exact construction-era and tree-canopy combination, guessing at a repair without seeing the actual pipe condition is a common way to spend money twice. A sewer camera inspection shows whether the issue is a single root mass at one joint, which usually means a straightforward spot repair, or root intrusion across multiple points along a fully degraded line, which points toward hydro jetting or a fuller trenchless replacement.

This distinction is not cosmetic. A homeowner who pays for a spot repair on a line that has root intrusion at five different joints will likely be back to square one within a year. Seeing the footage first is what prevents that.

Cabling Alone Is a Temporary Fix, Not a Solution

Standard cabling cuts a path through root mass and clears the immediate blockage, but it does not remove what is growing against or through the pipe wall. That is why the same line tends to clog again on a shorter and shorter cycle each time. Hydro jetting, which uses high-pressure water to clear the full diameter of the pipe rather than just punching a channel through it, is the more durable fix once a line has needed repeat cabling.

Real Cost Expectations for Root-Related Repairs

A straightforward spot repair for a single root-damaged joint typically runs $1,800 to $4,000. A line requiring trenchless replacement due to widespread degradation runs higher, often $5,000 to $9,500, depending on length and depth. Hydro jetting alone, without a structural repair, typically runs $350 to $800 and is often tried first once a camera inspection confirms the pipe itself is structurally sound and the issue is purely buildup and root mass rather than a collapsed or cracked section. Actual pricing always depends on the specific line's length, depth, and access difficulty.

The cost difference between these options is exactly why the camera inspection step matters so much. Committing to a full trenchless replacement without first confirming the actual extent of the damage risks significant overspending on a problem that a spot repair or jetting could have resolved.

Questions Rosemont and Beverley Hills Homeowners Ask

Will removing the trees solve the problem? Not reliably, and it is rarely worth the tradeoff. Root systems can persist and continue growing toward a damaged pipe joint for years after a tree is removed, and the mature tree canopy is part of what gives both neighborhoods their character and property values. Addressing the pipe itself is the more direct fix.

How often should an older home's sewer line be inspected? There is no universal schedule, but a home that has needed cabling even once is a reasonable candidate for a camera inspection, simply to rule out root intrusion before it becomes a repeat problem.

Does this affect home insurance or resale value? A documented, repaired sewer issue is generally viewed more favorably by both insurers and buyers than an undocumented, ongoing one. Addressing it directly, with camera documentation, tends to be the better long-term position either way.

What to Expect During the Repair Process

Once a camera inspection confirms the scope of root intrusion, the actual repair timeline depends heavily on which approach applies. A spot repair at a single damaged joint is typically a one-day job, often completed with minimal excavation limited to the exact damage location. Trenchless replacement, used when a line shows degradation across multiple points, takes longer, generally one to two days, but still avoids the full-yard excavation that an older-style dig-and-replace approach would require.

Either way, the property is left in a condition close to how it started, since both methods are specifically designed to minimize surface disruption to a neighborhood where landscaping and hardscaping, from Rosemont's original street trees to Beverley Hills' mature lawns, are part of what residents value about living there. Homeowners who have been through this process often say the disruption was far less than they expected going in, largely because neither method requires the kind of full-yard trench that older sewer repair techniques once did.
