How to Know If Your Alexandria Rowhouse Still Has Galvanized Pipe

By the Alexandria Pro Plumbing team · September 15, 2026
Talk to a plumber
(703) 912-0567
Call Now

Galvanized pipe was the standard water supply material in American homes for decades before copper and, later, PEX took over. In much of the country that transition happened gradually. In Alexandria's oldest neighborhoods, Old Town, Rosemont, and Del Ray in particular, a meaningful share of homes still have some or all of their original galvanized supply lines in place, simply because the pipe has not failed enough yet to force a full replacement.

This is worth taking seriously even if your home has never had an obvious plumbing problem. Galvanized pipe fails from the inside out, which means a home can look and function completely normally right up until a leak appears with no warning behind a wall or under a floor.

A Quick Way to Check

You do not need a professional inspection to get a first read on this. A few checks most homeowners can do themselves:

Self-check before calling a plumber

  • Look at an exposed pipe in the basement or crawlspace. Galvanized pipe is a dull gray color and is magnetic, meaning a refrigerator magnet will stick to it. Copper is reddish-brown and non-magnetic. PEX is flexible plastic, usually red, blue, or white.
  • Check your home's construction year. If your Old Town, Rosemont, or Del Ray home was built before 1960 and has not been fully repiped, galvanized pipe is the more likely default, not the exception.
  • Look for corrosion at visible fittings. Rust-colored buildup or a narrowed opening at a joint is a sign of internal corrosion, which is galvanized pipe's defining failure mode.
  • Check your water pressure at different fixtures. Corrosion narrows the inside diameter of galvanized pipe over time, which shows up as gradually declining pressure, especially at fixtures furthest from the main line.

Why This Actually Matters (And When to Call a Plumber)

Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside, which means the visible exterior can look fine while the interior diameter has narrowed significantly or developed a pinhole leak that has not yet broken through. This is different from a visible problem like a crack, which announces itself. Galvanized pipe failure tends to be a surprise, showing up as a sudden leak behind a wall or under a floor after decades of quiet corrosion.

The functional lifespan of galvanized pipe is generally cited as 40 to 60 years. A large share of Alexandria's original housing stock in Old Town, Rosemont, and Del Ray was built well before that window closed, which is exactly why these three neighborhoods generate more leak detection, slab leak, and general repair calls than newer parts of the city.

Partial Repiping Is More Common Than You Might Expect

One detail that surprises a lot of homeowners: a house that has been renovated does not necessarily mean the whole plumbing system was updated. A kitchen or bathroom remodel often replaces the supply lines feeding that specific room, while leaving the rest of the house on its original galvanized branches. This creates a genuinely mixed system, part modern PEX or copper, part original galvanized, which is common enough in these neighborhoods that a technician will typically ask about renovation history before assuming either way.

Repair Versus Full Repipe

A single galvanized leak does not automatically mean the whole house needs to be repiped. If the leak is isolated and the rest of the system is not showing symptoms, a targeted repair is often the right call. But once leaks start recurring across different rooms or sections within a relatively short period, that pattern usually means the whole system is reaching the end of its functional life at roughly the same time, since it was all installed in the same era. At that point, a full repipe tends to be more cost-effective than continuing to chase individual leaks as they appear.

What a Repipe Actually Costs

For homeowners who reach the point of needing a full repipe, cost is usually the first question. A whole-home repipe to copper or PEX typically runs $4,000 to $15,000 depending on home size, with PEX generally costing less in materials while offering faster installation in the tight wall cavities common in these older rowhouses. A single isolated repair, by contrast, typically runs $150 to $600, which is why most homeowners start there and only move to a full repipe once repairs become genuinely recurring.

Common Questions About Older Alexandria Plumbing

Is galvanized pipe actually dangerous? Not in the way lead pipe can be. The primary risk is mechanical failure, corrosion leading to reduced flow or a sudden leak, not a health hazard from the material itself. That said, severely corroded pipe can sometimes contribute to water quality issues like discoloration.

Can I repipe just part of my house? Yes, and this is common. A kitchen or bathroom renovation often includes repiping just that section, leaving the rest of the original galvanized system in place until it needs attention.

How disruptive is a full repipe? Most residential repipes take three to five days, often completed in phases so at least part of the home retains water access throughout the project. Some drywall access and patching is typically needed afterward.

Why Waiting Rarely Saves Money

A common instinct is to wait until a galvanized pipe actually fails before addressing it, since the pipe still technically works in the meantime. In practice, this approach tends to cost more over the long run, not less. Each individual leak repair carries its own service call and labor cost, and a home that experiences three separate leaks over two years has usually spent more in cumulative repair costs than a single planned repipe would have, without ever actually solving the underlying problem. The pipe that just leaked is not the only section reaching the end of its service life; it is simply the first one to fail, with the rest following on a similar timeline.

This is exactly the pattern that shows up most often in Old Town, Rosemont, and Del Ray's original housing stock, homes where the entire supply system was installed within the same few years now reaching the end of its functional lifespan together, not in isolation. Recognizing this pattern early, after the second leak rather than waiting for a fifth, is usually what separates a homeowner who plans a repipe on their own timeline from one who is forced into an emergency decision during an active leak.
