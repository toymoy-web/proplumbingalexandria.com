A sump pump is a technical name for something most homeowners only think about the moment it stops working: the small pump in a basement pit that keeps groundwater from flooding the space below grade. In Alexandria, that matters most in the low-lying blocks near the Potomac waterfront and in older homes with a history of basement moisture, where a failed pump during a storm can mean several inches of water within hours.

When the Basement Starts Filling With Water

Most calls on this page start the same way: water is rising in a basement or crawlspace and the homeowner doesn't yet know it's a sump pump problem, only that the floor is wet again after heavy rain. If the pit has a pump in it and the water keeps climbing anyway, that's a sump pump installation or repair call, not a general plumbing leak.

Local Cause

Why Sump Pumps Fail in Alexandria Homes

The area's naturally high water table near the Potomac puts continuous pressure on basement foundations, so a sump pump here runs far more often than one in a home on higher, drier ground. That extra duty cycle wears out float switches and motors faster, and a power outage during the same storm that's causing the water rise is a common way a working pump becomes a useless one at the worst possible time.

Signs to Watch For

Signs Your Sump Pump Needs Attention

Pump Runs Constantly

A pump cycling on and off every few minutes, even without rain, usually means a stuck float switch or an undersized unit.

Loud Grinding or Rattling

Unusual noise from the pit often signals a worn motor bearing or debris caught in the impeller.

Water in the Pit but No Pump Response

If the float visibly rises and the pump never kicks on, the switch or motor has likely failed.

No Battery Backup

A primary pump with no battery backup leaves the basement unprotected during the power outages that often accompany the storms that cause flooding.

How We Diagnose It

How We Diagnose a Failing Pump

A technician tests the float switch, checks the check valve for backflow, and confirms the discharge line isn't frozen or blocked before condemning the pump itself, since a pump that looks dead is sometimes a simple switch or valve fix. If the motor has failed or the unit is undersized for the pit's actual water volume, replacement with a correctly sized pump is usually the more durable fix than another repair.

Repair Options

Repair vs. Replacement

Repair

  • Lower upfront cost
  • Fixes switch, valve, or wiring issues
  • Keeps an otherwise sound pump
  • Best when the motor itself is healthy

Full Replacement

  • Correctly sized for the pit
  • Battery backup option added
  • Fewer failures during storms
  • Recommended for pumps 7+ years old

Before You Call

What to Do If Water Is Rising Now

Check the Power First

Confirm the pump is plugged in and the outlet has power, since a tripped GFCI outlet is a common, easy-to-miss cause.

Don't Stand in the Water

Avoid wading into a flooded basement near outlets or the electrical panel until power to the area is confirmed safe.

Note the Water Level

Mark or photograph how high the water has risen so the technician can judge how much capacity is needed going forward.
